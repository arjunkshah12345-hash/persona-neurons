How it works

The steering recipe comes from @_lyraaaa_. Pick a neuron n in the MLP of layer L. At every position, add a constant to that neuron's activation just before the down-projection:

act[n] += dose × median‖residL‖ / ‖Wdown[:, n]‖

Dividing by the size of the neuron's output weights makes doses comparable across neurons. At dose 1, the neuron writes a vector into the residual stream as large as a typical residual vector at that layer. Negative doses push the neuron the other way. Most of the good neurons worked between ±0.5 and ±1.

The hard part is finding the right neuron. Qwen3.5-4B has 32 layers of 9,216 MLP neurons each, and steering takes a generation run for every neuron and dose. We needed a cheap way to rank candidates, and a judge we could trust.

Attempt 1: which neurons fire differently?

For each of 16 personas (pirate, Shakespeare, sleepy, noir…), we had the model answer 24 questions with a persona system prompt. We then replayed those answers without the system prompt and ranked neurons by how differently they fired on persona text than on neutral answers. The model judged its own outputs.

This found a few real neurons, including a Shakespeare neuron (L2 N2712) and a cat neuron (L2 N7763). But it had two problems:

  • The self-judge was badly calibrated. It called an anxiety neuron "sarcastic" and rewarded outputs stuck repeating "menus menus menus".
  • Activation difference finds neurons that read a style, not ones that write it. A neuron that lights up on pirate text does not make the model talk like a pirate when you turn it up.

A better judge

We re-scored everything offline with Claude (Sonnet for finals, Haiku for the large screens). The judge sees batches of shuffled samples and rates each one from 0 to 10 on all 16 styles, plus whether it answers the question and whether it reads fluently. Rating every style at once also catches neurons that hit a style they weren't searched for. Two scores matter:

style = concept × fluent keep = concept × min(fluent, on_topic)

Re-judging the first run's screen showed that the ranking, not the judge, was the main bottleneck. For most styles, no candidate was any good.

Attempt 2: ask the gradient what writes the style

So we asked a direct question: which neuron, if turned up, makes the persona's answer more likely? We add a zero vector at every layer's down-projection input and backpropagate the log-probability of the persona answer (no system prompt) minus that of a neutral answer. One backward pass gives, for all 294,912 neurons at once, a first-order estimate of how a dose changes the gap between persona and neutral text.

We took 20 candidates per style, 3 doses each: 965 configurations, rated by Haiku. The best 3 neurons per style then went to finals at three doses each, with 16 answers per configuration rated by Sonnet: 1,608 rated answers in total.

What we noticed

  • Voice and topic are different neurons. Some neurons change how the model talks (Shakespeare, poet, hype). Others change what it talks about: the sleepy neuron often writes about sleep, and the cat neuron decides your squeaky door is a cat.
  • Early layers carry style, late layers push tokens. The top of the gradient ranking is crowded with late-layer neurons that just boost one token (for pirate, "arr"). Turned up, they repeat that word. The useful style neurons sit mostly in layers 2–12.
  • Some neurons are stranger than their label. L5 N7331 was found while searching for "melancholy". What it actually does is offer condolences for a loss you never mentioned, even when asked about refrigerators.
  • Every persona trades off against the answer. The neurons that stay most on topic (hype, Shakespeare, food) are also the subtler ones. Turn them up further and the answer goes first.

Limits

  • The judge is a language model. Claude's ratings are much better than the model judging itself, but they are still ratings. All samples and scores are in the repo, so you can read them yourself.
  • Small samples. Finals use 8 questions × 2 samples (temperature 0.7). Differences under about 0.05 in style score are noise.
  • First-order search. Gradient attribution is a linear estimate at dose 0, while real doses are large. It ranks candidates well, but it can miss neurons that only matter at high doses.
  • One model. Neuron indices are specific to Qwen/Qwen3.5-4B and will not transfer to other models.

Reproduce it

Each run is one Python script that runs on Kaggle's free 2× T4 GPUs: src/hunt3.py for the current version. Outputs are judged offline with src/rejudge.py, which calls the claude CLI. All samples and judge scores are in results/. See the README.
